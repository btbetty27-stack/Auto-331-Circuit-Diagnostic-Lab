🔍 DIAGNOSTICS TRAINER

Each circuit has been wired correctly but contains a single fault. Your job is to use the DVOM to diagnose the fault and identify its location.

Meter Modes: DC Voltage measures voltage in a live circuit. Resistance measures resistance with the circuit off. Continuity beeps when a path is complete. Use the mode buttons or click the dial.

Test Points: Click any yellow dot on the circuit to place a probe. Select RED or BLACK probe first, then click the test point.

Diagnosing: Click any component or wire segment on the circuit to select it as the fault location. Then click Submit Diagnosis.

Attempts: There is no limit on tries — keep testing until you find it. Getting it right on your 1st attempt earns 10 points, losing 1 point for each additional attempt, down to 0 points once you reach your 11th attempt. A hint is revealed after your 1st and 2nd incorrect attempts.

⚡ TYPES OF ELECTRICAL FAULTS

A quick reference for the four fault types you'll run into in these circuits — what's happening electrically, how the customer experiences it, what you'd actually see during a visual inspection, and how the fuse or circuit breaker reacts.

Fault Type What Happens App Symptoms (How It Acts) Visual Signs (What to Look For) Protective Device Reaction
Short to Ground A live positive wire touches the metal vehicle frame or ground wire before the load.
  • Component immediately stops working.
  • Replacement fuse blows immediately when power is applied.
  • Blown fuse (cloudy glass, broken element).
  • Melted wire insulation or smoke.
  • Scorch marks on the metal frame where the wire rubbed through.
 Fuse blows instantly or the automotive circuit breaker trips and resets repeatedly (or stays open if it's manual).
Short to Power A live positive wire accidentally touches another positive wire (usually from a different circuit).
  • A component stays turned on constantly.
  • Turning on one switch accidentally activates a completely different accessory (e.g., hitting the horn flashes a headlight).
  • Copper wires melted or fused together inside a wiring harness.
  • Damaged insulation exposing bare wire copper.
 Usually no reaction (unless total current exceeds the fuse rating), but causes ghost operations in other circuits.
Open Circuit A complete break in the path of the circuit, stopping all current flow.
  • Component is completely dead.
  • Fuse remains perfectly intact.
  • Operating the switch has absolutely no effect.
  • Severed or broken wire.
  • Disconnected harness plug.
  • Backed-out pins inside a connector terminal.
 No reaction. Fuses and breakers do not trip because current drops to zero.
Excessive Resistance Corrosion, loose connections, or undersized wire creates an unwanted restriction in the circuit.
  • Component operates weakly (e.g., dim lights, slow power windows, slow-cranking starter).
  • Circuit functions intermittently when shaken.
  • Green or white corrosion on battery terminals or ground points.
  • Loose, wobbly, or loose-fitting electrical connectors.
  • Discolored, heat-damaged plastic connector housings.
 Rarely trips. The high resistance actually reduces current flow below the fuse rating, turning the restriction into an accidental heater instead.

🔧 DIAGNOSTIC TESTING GUIDE

How to actually confirm each fault type with the DVOM — the meter setting to use, the exact procedure, and what a confirmed fault looks like versus a normal, healthy reading.

1. Short to Ground

The Goal: Find out if power is escaping straight to the vehicle frame before reaching the component.
Mode
Resistance / Ohms (Ω) or Continuity (🔊)
Red Lead
VΩmA jack
Black Lead
COM jack
  1. Remove the blown fuse.
  2. Connect the black tester lead to a clean metal part of the car frame (ground).
  3. Touch the red tester lead to the load side of the fuse terminal (the side going toward the component).
Fault Confirmed:0.00 Ω (or a continuous beep). This means there is a direct, zero-resistance path straight to the metal frame.
Normal Reading:OL (Open Loop) or very high resistance. With the switch open or component unplugged, there should be no path to ground here.

2. Short to Power

The Goal: Check if a wire is accidentally stealing electricity from a completely different circuit.
Mode
DC Voltage (V⎓, set to the 20V scale if not auto-ranging)
Red Lead
VΩmA jack
Black Lead
COM jack
  1. Turn the switch for the broken component OFF.
  2. Connect the black tester lead to a good chassis ground.
  3. Touch the red tester lead to the power input wire right at the component.
Fault Confirmed:12.0V to 14.4V. If you see full battery voltage when the switch is turned completely off, it is pulling power from a neighboring live wire.
Normal Reading:0.00V. There should be zero voltage at the component when its switch is off.

3. Open Circuit

The Goal: Locate where the wire pathway is physically broken.
Mode
Resistance / Ohms (Ω) or Continuity (🔊)
Red Lead
VΩmA jack
Black Lead
COM jack
  1. Disconnect power from the circuit.
  2. Disconnect both ends of the wire you want to test.
  3. Touch the red lead to one end of the wire and the black lead to the other end.
Fault Confirmed:OL (Open Loop / Out of Limits) and no beep. The multimeter cannot send its tiny test current from one end to the other because the path is physically broken.
Normal Reading:Close to 0.00 Ω (typically less than 0.5 Ω). A solid, healthy piece of copper wire has virtually zero resistance.

4. Excessive Resistance

The Goal: Find hidden corrosion or loose connections that are choking the electrical flow.
Mode
DC Voltage (V⎓) — perform a "Voltage Drop Test," which is much more accurate than testing ohms on a corroded circuit.
Red Lead
VΩmA jack
Black Lead
COM jack
  1. Keep the circuit completely plugged in and turned ON.
  2. Touch the red tester lead to one side of a circuit or component.
  3. Touch the black tester lead to the other side of the circuit or component.
Fault Confirmed:Anything above 0.5V. If the meter reads above .5V, in any part of the circuit besides the load, it means that much voltage is getting "lost" or trapped in the dirty connections along the way before it can reach the component.
Normal Reading:Less than 0.2V to 0.5V. A perfect wire drops almost no voltage.
